---
title: The Sino-Soviet split was a choice, not a structural inevitability
date: 2026-09-01
summary: A response to the claim that China was too weak to be an equal of the Soviet Union but too powerful to be a subordinate.
note: Written at LSE in 2026
draft: true
---

In May 1989, Deng Xiaoping met Mikhail Gorbachev to normalise Sino-Soviet relations. Deng, who
had personally delivered some of the harshest doctrinal attacks of the 1960s, accusing the
Soviets of great-power chauvinism and betrayal of Marxism-Leninism, now offered a revealing
reassessment: "we no longer think everything that we said at that time was right."[^1] When
asked about the split's cause, Deng suggested "the basic problem was that the Chinese were not
treated as equals and felt humiliated."[^2] This language of feeling and treatment, rather than
material capabilities, reframes the split as a question of intangible status and recognition.

The claim that China was too weak to be an equal of the Soviet Union, but too powerful to be a
subordinate, is deterministically constructed due to its binary composition that can only lead
to "equal" or "subordinate" relations, making rupture an inevitable outcome of China's position.
This article will dispute the claim and propose an alternative argument in three parts. First,
by disputing that China was "too weak to be an equal" based on its credentials and ideological
authority as a revolutionary force and a powerful player within the communist bloc. Second, by
disputing that China was "too powerful to be a subordinate" based on comparative case studies of
other nations and the temporal paradox of China itself, as evidenced by Liu Shaoqi explicitly
pledging the CCP would "submit to the decisions of the Soviet Communist party."[^3] Finally,
proposing an alternative argument that centres on Mao and his agency, asserting that Mao chose
ideological confrontation independent of any structural constraints. This is evidenced by his
conversation with Yudin and his preference for doctrinal warfare over quiet autonomy and the
connection made between domestic mobilisation and international relations. The rupture was not
caused by China being in an impossible position; it was caused by Mao consciously making it
impossible.

Donald Zagoria's, and other early interpretations, saw the split as a clash of national
interests, arguing diverging geopolitical priorities made rupture inevitable.[^4] The archival
turn of the 1990s and 2000s overturned this. Chen Jian showed that China and the Soviet Union
"had more shared national interests than ever" at the very moment disagreements emerged; the
split "was not caused by uncompromising conflicts over national interests but rather by
different understandings and interpretations of the same ideology."[^5] Lorenz Lüthi, working
through newly available Soviet, Chinese, and Eastern European archives, reached a similar
conclusion: "among all the causes, ideology was the most important."[^6] But even scholars who
foreground ideology often imply that ideological competition made rupture structurally
inevitable. Lüthi himself suggests that once Khrushchev's de-Stalinisation "let the genie of
ideological pluralism out of the bottle," the alliance was doomed.[^7] This is still
determinism, though ideological rather than material, mirroring the claim's binary logic.
Ideological competition created opportunities for rupture. It did not require Mao to seize them.

The claim captures something real about Sino-Soviet material asymmetry. China was economically
and militarily dependent on the Soviet Union throughout the 1950s. The Korean War intensified
this: Soviet share of China's foreign trade rose from 30% in 1950 to 56.3% by 1953.[^8] Over
10,000 Soviet specialists worked in China transferring technical knowledge, while Soviet loans
totalling 1.4 billion roubles financed economic assistance.[^9] When they were withdrawn in July
1960, two-thirds of Soviet-aided industrial projects remained incomplete.[^10] Lüthi observes
that "despite claims of equality within the socialist camp… the PRC was never equal to the
Soviet Union in either the socialist camp or in world affairs."[^11] By conventional measures,
China was unmistakably the junior partner.

Yet the claim measures power in the wrong currency. Economic dependence, military inferiority,
industrial backwardness: these are the metrics of conventional statecraft. They tell us little
about the communist world: Chen and Lüthi both argue that ideology, not material interest, drove
the split. Radchenko goes further, noting that the communist system "required hierarchy and
subordination" organised around ideological authority, not industrial output.[^12] In such a
system, power came from authority to interpret the ideology that legitimated rule. Thus, a claim
to interpretive authority was a claim to power as significant as any material capability.

By this metric, China was far from weak. The CCP had achieved power through indigenous
revolutionary struggle, at times against Moscow's wishes, rather than through Soviet occupation.
This distinction mattered because, where Poland's Gomulka and Hungary's Kadar owed their
positions to Moscow's approval, Mao owed his to twenty-two years of revolutionary warfare.[^13]
This revolutionary power extended beyond the communist bloc: from 1950, Beijing positioned
itself as mentor to Asian liberation movements, promising Ho Chi Minh "all the military
assistance Vietnam needed" and dispatching advisors to guide Viet Minh resistance to
France.[^14] Moreover, at Bandung in 1955, a conference from which Moscow was excluded, Chinese
diplomats built a united front with decolonising states. The Chinese model of peasant
mobilisation offered a path to revolution that Soviet industrial modernisation could not;
indeed, China's Third World influence pushed the Soviets towards more sustained commitments in
response.[^15] In this light it is difficult to accept that China was fundamentally "too weak to
be an equal."

Khrushchev's February 1956 Secret Speech destabilised communist orthodoxy, and if China was "too
weak to be an equal," this moment should have confirmed its marginality. Instead, it
demonstrated the opposite. With Stalin gone, Mao had become increasingly reluctant to respect
the authority of Stalin's weaker successor.[^16] On 5 April 1956, *Renmin Ribao* published "On
the Historical Experience of the Proletarian Dictatorship," offering an independent evaluation
of Stalin's legacy that argued he remained a "great Marxist-Leninist" despite his errors.[^17]
The Kremlin responded by admitting errors in its China policy and promising more aid to
China.[^18] This was not an isolated concession. At the 1954 Geneva Conference, Moscow had
pushed to have China invited as "an equal partner" to negotiate Asian conflicts, and Radchenko
observes that throughout this period "the Soviets often deferred to Chinese expertise,
especially in relation to Southeast Asia."[^19] Material weakness had not prevented Beijing from
exercising real authority within the communist world.

The 1956 Polish and Hungarian crises revealed how substantially Chinese standing had grown. When
upheaval threatened Soviet control over Eastern Europe, Khrushchev asked the Chinese for advice,
and Beijing dispatched a delegation to Moscow whose counsel influenced Soviet decisions on both
crises.[^20] The Soviet leadership's 30 October declaration proclaiming readiness to place
relations with socialist states on a basis of "sovereign equality" came partly at Chinese
urging; Radchenko notes that Mao "was unquestionably satisfied" when Khrushchev agreed to
publish it.[^21] That Moscow consulted Beijing on European matters demonstrated ideological
authority that material weakness alone cannot explain. At the November 1957 Moscow conference,
Mao declared that "the East Wind prevails over the West Wind" and publicly affirmed Soviet
leadership.[^22] Yet the act of affirmation revealed where authority lay: by endorsing Soviet
leadership, Mao "virtually had placed himself in the capacity of a judge from a higher court,
implying that it was he who now occupied a morally superior position," for to grant leadership
was to claim the standing to grant it.[^23] That standing came not from material capability but
from revolutionary credentials, credentials the Soviet leadership, increasingly defensive after
de-Stalinisation, could not easily dismiss. By 1957, China was not too weak to be an equal. In
ideological terms, it had become an equal whose recognition Soviet leadership increasingly
required.

Revolutionary origins and ideological authority established that China was not "too weak to be
an equal," but did this mean China was structurally "too powerful to be a subordinate"? If so,
the evidence should show consistent resistance to subordination regardless of circumstances.
However, instead, it reveals variation and a multitude of options presented to Mao to accept or
reject subordination rather than being structurally bound to confrontation.

Three case studies isolate different structural explanations. Albania tests whether weakness
guarantees subordination: tiny, impoverished, entirely dependent on external support. If
structure fixed outcomes, Albania should have been the most reliably subordinate satellite.
Instead, Mastny observes that "tiny Albania's brazen defiance of Soviet authority and its
defection" constituted "the first… attack on the integrity of the Warsaw Pact."[^24] One might
object that Albania could defy Moscow only because China offered an alternative patron, but this
concedes the point: Albania chose to defy Soviet leadership, confounding structural
explanations.

Poland tests whether negotiated autonomy was available. In October 1956, Gomulka was elected
First Secretary without Soviet consultation. Khrushchev flew to Warsaw uninvited, and Soviet
forces began converging on the city. Gomulka faced imminent invasion. He had been imprisoned
under Stalin; his populace was anti-Soviet, with deep historical grievances. Yet he negotiated
autonomy within acknowledged Soviet hegemony.[^25] Poland accepted continued Warsaw Pact
membership and deference to Soviet leadership of the international communist movement, gaining
domestic policy flexibility in return. If Poland, being weaker, with fewer bargaining chips and
less room to manoeuvre could secure such terms, China could surely have secured better ones. The
structural logic of "too powerful to be a subordinate" implies that subordination was impossible
for a state of China's size. Yet Poland's example suggests subordination was available; what
varied was its price. China's power should have made that price lower, not the arrangement
unattainable. That Mao found even favourable subordination unacceptable, demanding not autonomy
but equality, not flexibility but leadership, comes down to his decision to do so, not
structure.

Yugoslavia isolates revolutionary credentials. Like China, Tito's partisans achieved communist
rule through an indigenous struggle, not Soviet occupation. Thus, if revolutionary credentials
structurally necessitated confrontation, Yugoslavia should have followed a similar trajectory to
China. The 1948 Soviet-Yugoslav split certainly demonstrated Tito's willingness to assert
independence: signing separate treaties and refusing to share economic information with Moscow.
Yet crucially, Tito never bid for leadership of international communism, and after Stalin's
death, unlike Mao, Tito chose rapprochement with Khrushchev combined with non-alignment,
sustaining a high degree of autonomy without resorting to doctrinal warfare with Moscow.[^26]
One might object that Yugoslavia's smaller size made accommodation easier, but this again only
reinforces the point. If a weaker state with revolutionary credentials could sustain autonomy
through negotiation, a stronger one should have found favourable terms even more attainable.
Revolutionary credentials did not compel Mao towards confrontation; he chose it.

Temporal evidence further counters the notion of China being "too powerful to be a subordinate":
China itself made different choices at different moments. Its structural position (indigenous
revolution, strategic depth, no Soviet troops on its territory) remained constant throughout the
1950s. However, despite apparently being "too powerful to be a subordinate," from 1950 to 1957
Beijing cooperated with Moscow and accepted a subordinate role, however uncomfortably: Mao
complained that Stalin had made him "swallow bitter fruits" in 1950 negotiations.[^27] Resentment
did not prevent cooperation: Liu Shaoqi explicitly told Stalin in July 1949 that "the Soviet
Communist Party is the main headquarters of the international Communist movement, while the
Chinese Communist Party is just a battle-front headquarters… the CCP submits to the decisions of
the Soviet Communist Party" even, he added, "when it disagreed with them."[^28] This was not
mere diplomatic rhetoric: the 1950 Treaty of Friendship imposed "quasi-imperialist conditions,"
including joint-stock companies, joint railway ownership, and secret agreements closing
Manchuria and Xinjiang to all foreigners except Soviets.[^29] China is itself evidence that it
was not "too powerful to be a subordinate"; the shift towards confrontation came only in
1958-60, precisely when the Great Leap Forward had left it materially weaker, the inverse of
what structural logic would predict. Mao chose to stop subordinating China, in large part to
distract from domestic political backlash.

Having established that China was neither "too weak to be an equal" nor "too powerful to be a
subordinate," thereby disproving structural explanations, the question becomes: why did rupture
occur? The evidence points to choice over constrained necessity. Mao chose confrontation because
external conflict served domestic purposes at a moment when the catastrophic Great Leap Forward
had devastated his authority. Three bodies of evidence demonstrate this was deliberate strategy:
the July 1958 confrontation with Ambassador Yudin, the form the split took, and Mao's explicit
linkage of external conflict with internal mobilisation.

The timing of Mao's confrontation points to domestic motivation, and thereby personal agency.
Grain production fell from 200 million tons in 1958 to 143.5 million tons in 1960, and the
resulting famine killed between twenty and forty-five million people.[^30] At the July 1959
Lushan Conference, Defence Minister Peng Dehuai challenged Mao to evaluate the Great Leap's
failures; Mao purged him but emerged from the crisis weakened, even stepping back from
day-to-day leadership.[^31] It was at this moment of domestic vulnerability, not when Soviet
behaviour worsened, that Mao intensified external confrontation. Khrushchev had already mocked
communes publicly as "reactionary," providing a ready target: Chen argues that Mao exploited the
Soviet expert withdrawal to deflect blame for the Great Leap's catastrophe onto Moscow.[^32]

The Mao-Yudin conversation of 22 July 1958 is indicative of how Mao manufactured grievance from
reasonable proposals. Yudin, who was Soviet Ambassador to Beijing, had proposed a joint
submarine fleet, while the Soviet Defence Minister had also suggested jointly building
long-range radio stations on Hainan. Despite the fact that Zhou Enlai himself had requested
Soviet naval assistance just weeks earlier and that this would have materially strengthened
Chinese military capabilities, Mao transformed this into an insult.[^33] Mao recast this
practical cooperation as evidence that the Soviet Union "intended to control China," insisting
the attitude was racial: "To you, the Soviets are the first-class whereas the Chinese are among
the inferior who are dumb, careless, and untrustworthy."[^34] This framing forced a binary:
Moscow could either accept the charge of chauvinism or back down. Structural explanations cannot
account for why Mao transformed a helpful response to China's own request into an intolerable
insult. He assured Yudin that cooperation remained "still possible" in other areas, and insisted
Yudin report to Khrushchev "exactly what I have remarked without any polishing," but a leader
responding spontaneously would be unlikely to reassure or specify such stipulations.[^35] The
performance worked: Khrushchev flew to Beijing within days and capitulated. Mao had constrained
Soviet options despite China's material weakness, not by utilising any existing power, but by
constructing authority through confrontation itself.

The form of the rupture provides further evidence. If diverging material interests caused the
split, the conflict should have produced territorial confrontation or quiet estrangement.
Territorial disputes generate border incidents; interest conflicts produce hard bargaining and,
usually, eventual compromise. Instead, the Sino-Soviet split produced over 100,000 words of
doctrinal polemic: the Nine Letters of 1963-64 criticised Soviet ideology and extensively cited
Marx, Engels, Lenin, and Stalin. The polemic's opening letter made the intended audience
explicit: "we also hope this will be conducive to the understanding of our views by the
fraternal Parties."[^36] This was communication directed at the entire communist world,
challenging Moscow's traditional dominance. As Lüthi observes, "The Chairman had decided that he
wanted either a complete break with the CPSU or a complete Soviet surrender. Compromise was
simply not an option."[^37] "Decided" is the key word, for this was Mao's choice.

Mao did not merely use external tension for domestic purposes; he said he would. In 1958, he
declared that "besides its disadvantageous side, a tense [international] situation could mobilise
the population, could particularly mobilise the backward people, could mobilise the people in
the middle, and could therefore promote the Great Leap Forward in economic construction."[^38]
He then implemented this declared strategy. In December 1959, Mao denounced Peng Dehuai's
critique of the Great Leap as "a coup attempt supported by [his Soviet] friends."[^39] By
linking domestic critics to the external enemy, Mao could purge them as traitors rather than
loyal dissenters. The polemics served the same function. As Hu Qiaomu, a Politburo member
involved in drafting them, later acknowledged, they were "primarily aimed at the domestic
front."[^40] The second polemic defended Stalin's legacy, making the logic explicit: if
denouncing Stalin was revisionism, then denouncing Mao would be too. Mao chose confrontation
with Moscow because it enabled confrontation with domestic critics.

One might object that Mao's grievances were genuine, and he was genuinely humiliated by Soviet
slights. Lüthi argues that ideology functioned both as a genuine belief system and instrumental
device, both can be true.[^41] However, even genuine grievances require a decision to act on
them, and the fact that confrontation intensified when domestic crises made external enemies
useful, rather than when Soviet behaviour worsened suggests that Mao chose doctrinal
confrontation because it served purposes that accommodation could not.

Return to Deng Xiaoping's 1989 reflection: "we no longer think that everything we said at that
time was right." What had felt necessary in 1963 appeared in retrospect as choices that could
have been made differently, conflicts that need not have escalated. A structural explanation
would make such retrospective doubt meaningless, since no alternative was possible. That Deng
could imagine alternatives suggests he understood, as this article has argued, that the split
resulted from choices rather than constraints.

The claim frames China as too weak for equality yet too powerful for subordination. This
mistakes an outcome for a cause. Liu Shaoqi's 1949 pledge and seven years of cooperation
demonstrate that China was not structurally "too powerful to be a subordinate." China's
ideological authority from indigenous revolution, and accumulated through de-Stalinisation and
the 1956 crises, meant it was not "too weak to be an equal." China became too powerful to
subordinate because Mao's choices constructed a, primarily ideological, authority incompatible
with Soviet primacy, not because its position precluded this from the start.

Structure constrained but did not determine. De-Stalinisation created opportunities for Chinese
assertion; it did not compel Mao to exploit them through public doctrinal warfare. The Great
Leap's failures made external scapegoats useful; they did not require Mao to fuse domestic
purges with anti-Soviet campaigns. The split occurred not because China occupied an impossible
position but because Mao made choices that rendered accommodation impossible.
